For teachers and parents

What this site teaches, how to use it, and how it handles student privacy.

Purpose

Both Sides of the Byte is a self-paced learning site for grades 9–12. It gives students a clear, balanced picture of AI: how it works, how to use it well, what it costs people and the planet, and where it helps or harms. It aims to build judgment, not to push students toward or away from AI.

About the name and logo

A byte is 8 bits, split here into two halves of four. The green half stands for the benefits of AI and the pink half for its costs and risks. The small yellow dot is the balance point. The name reminds students to look at both sides of AI before they decide how to use it.

What students learn

  • How AI Works (4 lessons): machine learning, neural networks, and why chatbots can be confidently wrong.
  • AI Tools & Techniques (4 lessons): writing prompts, studying with AI, checking output, and choosing the right tool.
  • Ethical AI Use (7 lessons): academic integrity, bias, privacy, and AI’s energy, water, and hardware costs, plus ways to reduce them.
  • AI in the World (4 lessons): everyday AI, careers, deepfakes and the SIFT method, and a personal AI use plan.
  • The Good and the Bad (5 lessons): real benefits, real risks, stakeholders, weighing costs and benefits, and rules for AI.

Each module ends with a 5-question quiz, there are five hands-on activities, and a 15-question final exam leads to a printable certificate. Allow roughly 15–25 minutes per module (an estimate).

Ideas for the classroom

  • Assign one module per week and discuss one lesson each class.
  • Have students do the activities in pairs and compare their reasoning.
  • Use the “What would you do?” scenarios as discussion starters.
  • Ask students to write their own AI use plan after Module 4, then revise it after Module 5.

Discussion questions

  • When is it fair to say you “used” AI on an assignment? What should be disclosed?
  • Who benefits from AI in your school, and who might be left out or harmed?
  • Is a small personal AI footprint worth worrying about if companies make most of the choices?
  • What evidence would change your mind about whether a particular AI use is worth it?

Learning goals

The content connects to the AI4K12 “Five Big Ideas in AI,” especially Learning, Natural Interaction, and Societal Impact. Teachers should confirm alignment with their own district standards.

Privacy and safety

  • There are no accounts, logins, or forms that collect personal information.
  • Progress, XP, and badges are stored only in the browser on the student’s own device. Clearing browser data or using the Reset button removes them.
  • The certificate name is typed on the page and is not saved or sent anywhere.
  • The site loads two fonts from Google Fonts, so Google’s servers receive a standard web request when a page loads.
  • There are no ads, tracking, or chat features.

A note on accuracy

AI technology and its environmental numbers change quickly, and estimates differ between studies. The lessons say so where it matters and list their sources on the References page. Encourage students to check claims, which is one of the habits the site teaches.

Conversation starters for families

  • Ask your student to show you the “Spot the hallucination” activity.
  • Talk about your family’s rules for what is safe to type into an AI tool.
  • Ask which of the benefits and risks of AI they think matter most, and why.
